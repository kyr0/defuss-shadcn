    <h2 id="p2-abstract">Abstract</h2>
    <p>Book typography set its text by rules a compositor applied by hand: justified columns, hyphenation, an indent instead of a blank line, an initial at the opening, small capitals for its first line, ligatures and old-style figures. Each rule now has a CSS property. We collect them in one typesetting block and show that a two-column page set with them reads like print on a wide screen and like a book page on a phone.</p>
  </section>

  <div class="typeset paper-wide" lang="en" data-columns="2" data-rule data-align="justify" data-indent data-initial="drop" data-lead="smallcaps" data-ligatures="classic">
    <h2 class="h3 typeset-span"><span class="paper-num">1</span> The column</h2>
    <p>A printed column is a measure of even grey. The compositor justified each line by adding space between words and, where the space grew too wide, broke a word at a syllable. On the web the same work is done by text-align: justify and hyphens: auto, provided the block names its language; the browser then hyphenates by the rules of that language and keeps the last line of each paragraph flush to the start.</p>
    <p>Two columns halve the measure and keep the line within the sixty to seventy characters a reader takes in at a glance. The column property takes a count and a minimum width; when the page grows too narrow for two columns of that width, the block sets one, and the phone reads a single column without a separate layout.</p>
    <h2 class="h3 typeset-span"><span class="paper-num">2</span> The paragraph</h2>
    <p>A book separates its paragraphs by an indent of one em, not by a blank line: the column stays whole, and the eye finds a new thought by its first line alone. The first paragraph after a heading starts flush - the heading has already opened it. The initial-letter property sinks the first letter of the opening paragraph three lines deep, and the first line follows in small capitals.</p>

    <h2 class="h3 typeset-span"><span class="paper-num">3</span> The letter</h2>
    <p>Ligatures such as fi, fl and ffi, discretionary ones such as ct and st, and old-style figures that sit in the line like lowercase letters - 1450, 1469, 2026 - render where the font carries them; a font without the features sets the plain forms and loses nothing else.</p>
